Statistics and Experimentation for Data Science

Hypothesis testing provides a formal framework for deciding whether an observed effect (e.g., a lift in conversion rate from an A/B test) is likely to be real or could plausibly be explained by random chance, using a null hypothesis, a test statistic, and a p-value threshold (commonly 0.05) to decide significance.

A/B testing (randomized controlled experiments) is the gold standard for establishing causal impact of a product change, because random assignment to control and treatment groups breaks the link between the treatment and any confounding variables, which observational data alone cannot do.

Confidence intervals communicate the uncertainty around an estimate (e.g., a 95% confidence interval for average revenue per user) and are often more informative for decision-making than a single point estimate or a binary "significant/not significant" verdict.

Common statistical pitfalls include p-hacking (testing many hypotheses until one appears significant by chance), Simpson's paradox (a trend reversing when data is aggregated vs. segmented), and confusing correlation with causation, all of which a data scientist must guard against when communicating results to stakeholders.

Sample size and statistical power determine whether an experiment is capable of detecting an effect of a given size; underpowered experiments risk concluding "no effect" when a real, smaller effect actually exists but wasn't detectable given the sample size.
